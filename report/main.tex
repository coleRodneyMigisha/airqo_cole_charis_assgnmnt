\title{Forecasting Hourly \pmtwo{} from Sparse Low-Cost Sensor Records in Uganda: Gap-Aware Imputation, Time-Aware Validation and Cloud Deployment}
\author{Charis Opol\authorrefmark{1}\,\orcidicon{https://orcid.org/0009-0003-7417-7984}, Cole Rodney Migisha\authorrefmark{1}\,\orcidicon{https://orcid.org/0009-0000-5311-2485}}
\address[1]{Department of Computing and Technology, Faculty of Engineering, Design and Technology, Uganda Christian University, Mukono, Uganda (e-mail: b29654@students.ucu.ac.ug; b29657@students.ucu.ac.ug)}
\corresp{Corresponding author: Charis Opol (e-mail: b29654@students.ucu.ac.ug).}

\begin{abstract}
Low-cost sensor networks such as AirQo have expanded \pmtwo{} monitoring in Ugandan cities, but intermittent device availability leaves short, fragmented records, and it is unclear how well such records support forecasting. We develop and deploy a pipeline that predicts hourly \pmtwo{} from 14{,}600 records of 149 AirQo sensors, which contain readings on only eight days in three bursts. \pmten{} was excluded as an input because it is a same-hour co-measurement of the same particles rather than a driver of \pmtwo{}, and is unavailable in advance. Extreme readings were replaced by the nearest normal value from the same sensor, and weather gaps were filled by windowed interpolation, time-of-day profiles or a BRITS-style bidirectional LSTM. Twelve models were compared under chronological hold-out, expanding-window and leave-sensors-out validation; SMOTE was tested and rejected. From weather, time and sensor identity alone, a tuned support vector regressor achieved a hold-out MAE of 9.34\ugm{} ($R^2 = 0.34$), against 17.60\ugm{} for a mean baseline; adding the previous three hours of \pmtwo{} reduced the MAE to 8.43\ugm{} (LightGBM, $R^2 = 0.42$). The bidirectional imputer reduced imputation error by 21\% but changed prediction error by only $-0.4$\%. On a later full-month dataset (August 2026), the history-based models retained skill (MAE 13.70 against 19.21\ugm), whereas the weather-only models performed close to the baseline, consistent with drift in six of nine input features. The forecast model is deployed as a cloud API with a dashboard. In sparse networks, recent pollutant history matters more than weather or sophisticated imputation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\PARstart{F}{ine} particulate matter, written \pmtwo{}, is the mass of airborne particles smaller than 2.5 micrometres in diameter per cubic metre of air. Particles this small travel deep into the lungs, which is why \pmtwo{} is the pollutant of greatest health concern in most cities. Its coarser relative, \pmten{}, counts all particles smaller than 10 micrometres, so every particle in \pmtwo{} is also part of \pmten{}. The assignment brief cites a four-year reference-grade study (2018--2021) that found Kampala's annual mean \pmtwo{} to be 39\ugm{}, roughly eight times the World Health Organization (WHO) annual guideline of 5\ugm{}~\cite{who2021}, with peaks in the dry seasons and during the morning and evening traffic hours.

Measuring \pmtwo{} properly is expensive. A \emph{reference-grade} instrument, such as a Beta Attenuation Monitor (BAM), which collects particles on a filter tape and weighs them by how much beta radiation they absorb, costs far more than most African cities can deploy widely. AirQo, based at Makerere University, fills that gap with \emph{low-cost sensors}: small optical devices that estimate particle concentration from scattered light, built locally and placed across Ugandan cities~\cite{okure2022,sserunjogi2022}. The price of that coverage is accuracy and continuity. Low-cost sensors drift with temperature and humidity, so their readings have to be \emph{calibrated}, that is, statistically corrected against a reference instrument~\cite{adong2022}, and devices go offline for hours or days at a time.

Our task, set by the course brief, was to build a pipeline that takes raw AirQo sensor data all the way to a trained, evaluated, monitored and cloud-deployed model that predicts hourly \pmtwo{}. When we opened the data, one fact reshaped the project: although the file is labelled January to July 2026, it contains genuine readings on only eight days. This paper is therefore as much about what can be learned from a very short, fragmented record as it is about any one model.

The work went through two complete pipelines. The first used \pmten{} as an input and looked excellent on paper; examining \emph{why} it looked excellent led us to rebuild it without \pmten{}. Along the way we tried several ideas that did not survive testing, and we report them, because they explain why the final pipeline looks the way it does. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item an honest account of the two pipelines, the alternatives we tried and the evidence behind each decision (Sections~\ref{sec:phase1}--\ref{sec:phase2}, Table~\ref{tab:decisions});
\item a comparison of three gap-filling methods, including a bidirectional recurrent imputer, judged both on hidden values and on their effect on prediction (Section~\ref{sec:impeval});
\item a comparison of twelve models under three validation setups and on a later, full-month dataset (Sections~\ref{sec:results} and~\ref{sec:second});
\item drift monitoring with Evidently AI and a working cloud deployment, with the deployment routes that failed (Sections~\ref{sec:monitoring} and~\ref{sec:deploy}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{The AirQo network.} Okure \emph{et al.}~\cite{okure2022} characterised ambient air quality in Ugandan urban areas with AirQo's low-cost sensors, and Sserunjogi \emph{et al.}~\cite{sserunjogi2022} published an hourly \pmtwo{} and \pmten{} dataset from 68 AirQo monitoring stations for 2019--2020. Both describe the features we met in our data: uneven completeness and intermittent device availability.

\textbf{How far low-cost sensors can be trusted.} Adong \emph{et al.}~\cite{adong2022} calibrated AirQo sensors against a collocated BAM with ten machine-learning methods; a Random Forest lowered the \pmtwo{} root mean squared error from 18.6 to 7.2\ugm{}, and the calibration was rolled out to more than 120 devices. Lsoto \emph{et al.}~\cite{lsoto2026} compared 11 low-cost sensors with a BAM in Kampala over 13 months and found that the sensors followed the BAM at lower concentrations but spread out at higher values, and agreed better on 24-hour averages than hourly. Our data are AirQo's calibrated values, but these studies warn that hourly readings, especially high ones, still carry sensor error.

\textbf{Weather and \pmtwo{}.} Weather changes how particles form, grow, disperse and are washed out. Wang and Ogawa~\cite{wangogawa2015} found \pmtwo{} negatively correlated with temperature in Nagasaki, with humidity effects that change direction at a threshold. Yang \emph{et al.}~\cite{yang2017} found that across Chinese cities humidity was positively correlated with \pmtwo{} in North China and negatively in most other regions, and that the temperature relationship changed sign between autumn and winter. Closest to our setting, Wabinyai \emph{et al.}~\cite{wabinyai2026} modelled daily \pmtwo{} in Kampala and Fort Portal with meteorological variables and the previous day's \pmtwo{}; their models explained up to 57\% and 80\% of daily variability, and adding the lagged \pmtwo{} improved accuracy significantly.

\textbf{Machine-learning forecasts of \pmtwo{}.} Febrian and Permai~\cite{febrian2025} forecast daily \pmtwo{} in Jakarta and concluded that historical pollutant readings alone were sufficient for short-term forecasting, with meteorology adding little. Niyonzima \emph{et al.}~\cite{niyonzima2026} forecast \pmtwo{} one hour ahead on 759{,}480 hourly AirQo observations from 68 sites with an attention-based recurrent network, reaching a mean absolute error of 8.71\ugm{} and $R^2 = 0.68$, and also tested on held-out stations. G\"urtepe \emph{et al.}~\cite{gurtepe2025} predicted \pmtwo{} regionally with gradient-boosted trees and reported a ``station-blinded'' validation, which scores the model at stations it never saw (RMSE 9.21\ugm{}, against 8.28\ugm{} in their temporal evaluation).

\textbf{Methods we borrowed.} For gap filling we followed BRITS~\cite{cao2018}, a bidirectional recurrent imputer; for rare high-pollution hours we tested SMOTE~\cite{chawla2002} in its regression form~\cite{torgo2013}, which has been used for \pmtwo{} classes before~\cite{saminathan2023}; and for explanations we used SHAP~\cite{lundberg2017,lundberg2020}.

\textbf{The gap.} The studies above work with months or years of data. None asks what can be done when a network's record is eight days long. That is the question this project forced on us.

\begin{abstract}
We built a complete pipeline that predicts hourly PM2.5 concentration from AirQo low-cost sensor data: data preparation, exploratory analysis, model training, evaluation, explainability, an external test on a second dataset, August 2026 forecasts, monitoring with Evidently AI, and deployment as a web service on Render with a dashboard on Vercel. The supplied data contains 14{,}600 hourly records from 149 sensors, but readings exist on only eight days (1--3 January, 1--2 April and 1--3 June 2026). PM10 was removed from the pipeline: PM2.5 is the fine fraction of PM10, so the two rise and fall together because they are the same particles measured by the same instrument, not because PM10 drives PM2.5, and the literature points to meteorological conditions such as temperature and humidity as drivers of PM2.5 concentrations. PM2.5 is therefore predicted from temperature, humidity, time and sensor identity. Gaps are filled by windowed interpolation and time-of-day imputation, and a bidirectional LSTM imputer in the style of BRITS was added and tested. Extreme PM2.5 readings (1.5\% of readings) are replaced by the nearest normal reading of the same sensor. SMOTE was tested on the training data and left out because it made every model less accurate. Thirteen models were compared under three validation setups. The best forecast model, a tuned SVR, reaches MAE 9.34\ugm{} ($R^2$ 0.34) on the held-out June days. Adding the PM2.5 readings of the previous three hours to the tree models lowers this to 8.43\ugm{} ($R^2$ 0.42) and makes the models far more stable, but such models need recent readings and cannot forecast weeks ahead. On an external dataset covering all of August for 125 of the sensors, the forecast models are no better than predicting the training mean, while the models with PM2.5 history keep part of their skill (MAE 13.7\ugm). The bidirectional imputer is the most accurate imputer for gaps of six hours or more, but it does not improve the prediction models. With PM10 as an input the earlier version of the pipeline scored MAE 3.10\ugm; that accuracy came from a co-measurement of the same particles, which is also unavailable ahead of time, not from what drives PM2.5.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

AirQo, based at Makerere University, operates a network of locally manufactured low-cost air-quality monitors across African cities. Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) is the pollutant of greatest health concern: the particles penetrate deep into the lungs and bloodstream, and a four-year reference-grade study cited in the assignment brief found Kampala's annual average to be about 39\ugm{}, roughly eight times the WHO annual guideline of 5\ugm{}~\cite{who2021}. \subsection{What changed after the presentation}
\label{sec:changes}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{PM10 removed} from every table, plot, feature set and model, because it is a co-measurement of PM2.5 rather than a driver of it (Section~\ref{sec:evolution}).
\item \textbf{Sensors profiled by offline time} and classed as data-rich or data-poor.
\item \textbf{Gap filling in two steps:} windowed interpolation, then time-of-day imputation; plus a \textbf{bidirectional LSTM imputer} (BRITS-style) proposed by one of us.
\item \textbf{Extreme PM2.5 values} replaced by the nearest normal reading of the same sensor.
\item \textbf{SMOTE} tested on the training split, documented, and left out of the final models.
\item \textbf{New models:} LightGBM, an LSTM, and tree models that also use the PM2.5 readings of the previous hours.
\item \textbf{Three validation setups} instead of one.
\item \textbf{New evaluation:} masked-gap imputation test, SHAP explanations, sensor maps, and an external test on a second dataset.
\item \textbf{Plots revised:} calendars coloured by PM2.5 category, density plots instead of histograms, one combined time-series figure per site, and no AUC (it is not a regression metric).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Project evolution and key decisions}
\label{sec:evolution}

The project went through two pipelines. This subsection tells that story in order, including the ideas that were tried and dropped, because the dropped ideas explain the shape of the final pipeline. Table~\ref{tab:decisions} summarises the decisions.

\textbf{Phase 1: the first pipeline, with PM10.} Zeros were converted to missing values, \texttt{device\_name} was used as the sensor key, and coordinates were filled with the median of each site's own valid coordinates. Each sensor was reindexed to an hourly grid (758{,}112 sensor-hours) with an \texttt{is\_offline} flag, and the predictors (temperature, humidity and PM10) were interpolated only across interior gaps of at most three hours; PM2.5 was never interpolated. The data were split chronologically (11{,}633 training rows up to 1 June, 2{,}642 test rows on 2--3 June), and the target was converted to a Z-score fitted on training rows only (mean 34.49, standard deviation 31.70\ugm). Ridge, Linear, Polynomial, Random Forest, SVR and KNN models were tuned with \texttt{TimeSeriesSplit}. The selected tuned Random Forest reached MAE 3.10\ugm, RMSE 7.00\ugm{} and $R^2$ 0.898 on the test days, with 86.2\% of hours in the correct AQI category (the untuned Random Forest: MAE 3.27\ugm). PM10 carried about three quarters (76\%) of that model's feature importance, while temperature and humidity carried about 1\% each.

\textbf{Why Phase 1 was abandoned.} There are two reasons, one scientific and one practical.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{PM10 is not a driver of PM2.5.} PM2.5 is the fine fraction of PM10: every particle counted in PM2.5 is also counted in PM10, and the two are measured by the same instrument in the same hour. They are strongly correlated because they share the same particles and the same sources, not because PM10 causes PM2.5. A model that uses PM10 learns this overlap, not how PM2.5 comes about. The literature on what drives PM2.5 concentrations points instead to emissions and to meteorological conditions: temperature and humidity affect how particles form, grow, disperse and are removed from the air~\cite{wangogawa2015,yang2017,wang2019} (Section~\ref{sec:corr}). We therefore model PM2.5 from those conditions, together with time and sensor identity.
\item \textbf{PM10 is not known in advance.} Because it is measured at the same moment as the target, a model that needs it cannot forecast. In the August forecast PM10 had to be replaced by each sensor's typical value for the hour, so the forecasts only repeated each site's usual daily pattern.
\end{itemize}
Most of Phase~1's accuracy came from this overlap, so the drop in accuracy after removing PM10 (Section~\ref{sec:results}) is expected. Following the feedback on our presentation, PM10 was removed from every stage.

\textbf{Ideas tried and rejected, with the evidence.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{KNN imputation of missing coordinates.} Six sites never report a valid coordinate. An early version borrowed coordinates from the five sites with the most similar temperature, humidity and pollution profile. This was rejected: a similar pollution profile does not mean a nearby location, so the six sites are left without coordinates and off the maps.
\item \textbf{KNN filling of long gaps.} An early version filled every gap up to the last observation with a KNN imputer. For the five EDA sites 96--98\% of each hourly series became imputed, and only about 12\% of the values were distinct, because the imputer reused the same few real readings for every hour with the same hour, weekday and month. The result was an artificial repeating sawtooth in the daily means. Long gaps are now left missing for PM2.5 and are filled for weather only, by the time-of-day method (Section~\ref{sec:tod}).
\item \textbf{Zero handling for weather dropouts.} In Phase~1, treating the 0\,\textdegree C / 0\% dropouts as missing, keeping them as 0, or adding a dropout flag changed the Random Forest's test MAE only between 3.24 and 3.30\ugm, because PM10 dominated. The comparison no longer applies without PM10; the dropouts are now treated as missing and filled by the two-step gap filling.
\item \textbf{Latitude and longitude as predictors.} They were removed: \texttt{device\_name} already identifies the location, and six sensors have no coordinates.
\item \textbf{SMOTE.} Tested in Phase~2 on the training split only. It made all eight models worse on the hold-out (Section~\ref{sec:smote}), so \texttt{USE\_SMOTE = False}; the experiment stays in the notebook.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Phase 2: the rebuilt pipeline.} PM10 was dropped at loading. Extreme PM2.5 readings are replaced by the nearest normal reading of the same sensor (Section~\ref{sec:extreme}). Weather gaps are filled by windowed interpolation and then by a time-of-day profile with a local offset, both fitted on training rows only; a BRITS-style bidirectional LSTM imputer~\cite{cao2018} was added and evaluated as a third method. Recent PM2.5 readings were added as features for the ``nowcast'' models, LightGBM and two LSTMs were added, and every model is scored under three validation setups.

\textbf{The bidirectional LSTM imputer.} It is the most accurate imputer on hidden values (21\% lower error than the time-of-day method on average), but training the prediction models on its values changed their MAE by only $-0.4\%$ on average. The final models therefore keep the time-of-day imputation; the evidence is in Section~\ref{sec:impeval}.

\section{Phase 1: A First Pipeline, With \pmten{}}
\label{sec:phase1}

Our first pipeline followed the brief closely and used every measurement available, including \pmten{}. We describe it in the order we built it, including the ideas we later dropped.

\subsection{Cleaning and the ideas we tried}
In our first pass we standardised the data types and left the zeros in place; we then adopted the brief's rule and treated zeros as missing. Timestamps were parsed as UTC, and \texttt{device\_name} became the sensor key.

\textbf{Coordinates.} Missing coordinates were filled with the median of each site's own valid coordinates. Six sites never report a valid coordinate. We first tried \emph{k-nearest-neighbour (KNN) imputation} for them: a KNN imputer fills a missing value from the $k$ most similar records, here the five sites with the most similar temperature, humidity and pollution profile. We dropped it on reflection: two sites can have similar air without being close together, so the imputer would invent locations. The six sites are left without coordinates.

\textbf{An hourly grid.} \emph{Resampling} each sensor onto a complete hourly grid from 1 January to 31 July (149 sensors $\times$ 5{,}088 hours $=$ 758{,}112 sensor-hours) made every missing hour explicit, with an \texttt{is\_offline} flag. \emph{Interpolation}, drawing a straight line between the readings on either side of a gap, was allowed only for the predictors (temperature, humidity and \pmten{}) and only across gaps of at most three hours. \pmtwo{} was never interpolated, because it is the value being predicted.

\textbf{Filling the long gaps.} Curious whether the long gaps could be filled too, we applied a KNN imputer that matched each missing hour to hours with the same hour of day, weekday and month. The daily means of the result showed a suspiciously regular sawtooth. When we measured it, 96--98\% of each EDA sensor's hourly series had become imputed, and only about 12\% of its values were distinct: the imputer was copying the same few real readings into every matching hour. We reversed the decision and left long gaps missing.

\subsection{Modelling}
The data were split \emph{chronologically}: the model is trained on earlier hours and tested on later ones, as it would be used. Training covered 1 January--1 June (11{,}633 rows) and testing 2--3 June (2{,}642 rows). The target was converted to a \emph{Z-score}, the number of standard deviations above the mean, using the training mean (34.49\ugm) and standard deviation (31.70\ugm) only, so that nothing about the test period leaked into training. Ridge, Linear, Polynomial, Random Forest, support vector and KNN regressors (Section~\ref{sec:models}) were tuned with \texttt{TimeSeriesSplit}, a cross-validation scheme that always validates on hours later than those it trains on.

The tuned Random Forest was selected. On the test days it scored a mean absolute error (MAE) of 3.10\ugm{}, a root mean squared error (RMSE) of 7.00\ugm{} and $R^2 = 0.898$ (these metrics are defined in Section~\ref{sec:valid}), and put 86.2\% of test hours in the correct AQI category; the untuned Random Forest scored MAE 3.27\ugm{}. We also asked whether the weather dropouts mattered: treating them as missing, keeping them as 0, or adding a dropout flag changed the test MAE only between 3.24 and 3.30\ugm{}. The reason became clear from the feature importance: \pmten{} carried about 76\% of the model's importance, temperature and humidity about 1\% each.

\subsection{Phase 1 monitoring and forecasts}
Evidently AI (Section~\ref{sec:monitoring}) flagged 6 of the 7 columns it scored as drifted; those were the time features and the sensor identity, and temperature, humidity and \pmten{} were not among the scored columns. The drift reflected the make-up of the two windows, different months, weekdays and active sensors, rather than any change in the air. For the August forecasts, the unknown future weather and \pmten{} were replaced by each sensor's typical value for that hour of day.

\subsection{The turning point: why \pmten{} had to go}
\label{sec:turning}
A 3.10\ugm{} error looked excellent, so we asked what the model was actually using. The answer was \pmten{}, and two problems followed.

First, \pmten{} is not a \emph{driver} of \pmtwo{}. Because \pmtwo{} is the fine part of \pmten{}, the two are measured from the same particles, by the same instrument, in the same hour, and they rise and fall together because they share sources, not because one causes the other. A model that uses \pmten{} learns this overlap, not how \pmtwo{} comes about. The literature on what drives \pmtwo{} points instead to emissions and to weather~\cite{wangogawa2015,yang2017,wabinyai2026}.

Second, \pmten{} is not known in advance, so a model that needs it cannot forecast. In the August forecast \pmten{} had to be replaced by a typical value, and the forecasts simply repeated each site's usual daily pattern.

After the feedback on our presentation we removed \pmten{} from every stage and rebuilt the pipeline. We expected, and accepted, a large drop in accuracy.

\section{Data preparation}

\subsection{Approach and supporting literature}

The preparation follows the general workflow of Febrian and Permai~\cite{febrian2025}: standardise null values, quantify missingness, explore distributions and outliers, analyse correlations, impute where justified, normalise, and split chronologically. AirQo's own studies describe the characteristics we found in this data: intermittent device availability, varying data completeness, and calibrated values from low-cost sensors~\cite{okure2022,sserunjogi2022}. PM2.5 \emph{targets} are never imputed for training or scoring, because that would create artificial labels.

\subsection{Cleaning}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{PM10} is dropped immediately after loading, because it is a co-measurement of the same particles rather than a driver of PM2.5 (Section~\ref{sec:evolution}).
\item \textbf{Zeros} in temperature, humidity, PM2.5, latitude and longitude are converted to missing. The zero temperature and humidity readings (about 4{,}100 rows) are a weather-sensor dropout: every zero temperature coincides with a zero humidity, they come in long unbroken blocks, and they occur only on AirQo devices.
\item \textbf{Types:} timestamps are parsed as UTC, and \texttt{device\_name} is used as the sensor identifier because \texttt{site\_id} is missing in about half the rows.
\item \textbf{Empty sensors:} a sensor is removed if it has no valid reading at all or no valid PM2.5 reading. None met this rule, so none was dropped. Nine sensors report PM2.5 but never report weather; they were kept and their weather is imputed.
\item \textbf{Coordinates} are filled with the median of the site's own valid coordinates. Six sensors have no valid coordinate and are left without one.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Three ways of filling weather gaps}
\label{sec:imputers}
\emph{Imputation} means estimating a missing value. We imputed only the inputs (temperature and humidity); \pmtwo{} targets are never imputed for training or scoring, because a model would then be scored against values another model invented. We built three methods, each a step up in ambition.

\textbf{(a) Windowed interpolation.} A gap is filled by a straight line only when it is at most three hours long, has real readings on both sides, and the 24-hour window centred on it holds at least six real readings. It filled 555 temperature and 551 humidity values; longer gaps stay empty.

\textbf{(b) Time-of-day profile with a local offset.} Weather follows a daily cycle, so for each sensor we computed the mean of each variable for every hour of the day, falling back to the sensor's network and then to all sensors when it had too few readings. A missing hour gets that profile value plus an offset measured from the sensor's own readings within 12 hours, which captures whether that day ran warmer or cooler than usual. For the models the profiles are refitted on training rows only.

\textbf{(c) A BRITS-style bidirectional LSTM.} One of us proposed a learned imputer, and we implemented one in the style of BRITS~\cite{cao2018}. A \emph{recurrent neural network} reads a sequence one step at a time and carries a memory, its hidden state, from step to step; a \emph{long short-term memory} (LSTM) network~\cite{hochreiter1997} is a recurrent network with gates that decide what to keep and forget, which lets it remember over longer spans. A \emph{bidirectional} model runs one LSTM forwards in time and one backwards, so each missing hour is estimated from both its past and its future. As in BRITS, each direction estimates every value before it sees it, its memory fades with the time since the last real reading, and a \emph{consistency loss}, an extra training penalty when the two directions disagree, pulls their estimates together (weight 0.1). This is ordinary supervised learning with an added penalty, not reinforcement learning. Our version adds the hour of day and the sensor's offline share as always-known inputs, models \pmtwo{} on a log scale, hides blocks of 1 to 12 hours during training so that the model practises on realistic gaps, and drops BRITS's classification part. It is written in TensorFlow and is retrained, with its scalers, for every split.

\subsection{Features}
The model inputs are temperature and humidity; the hour of day, day of week and month; and the sensor identity. Time is \emph{cyclical}: 23:00 is next to 00:00, and December next to January, which a plain number hides. We therefore encode each time variable as a sine and cosine pair, for example $\sin(2\pi\,\mathrm{hour}/24)$ and $\cos(2\pi\,\mathrm{hour}/24)$. The device name is \emph{one-hot encoded}, turned into one yes/no column per sensor, so a model can learn a separate level for each sensor. Coordinates are not used, because the device already identifies the location.

For a second family of models we added \pmtwo{} \emph{history}: the same sensor's readings one, two and three hours earlier, their mean and how many of them exist. Only real earlier readings are used, so the value being predicted cannot appear in its own inputs. We call models with these features \emph{nowcast} models, since they estimate the present from the recent past and need live readings, and models without them \emph{forecast} models, since they can predict any future hour from the calendar and the weather.

\subsection{An experiment with SMOTE}
\label{sec:smote}
Unhealthy hours are rare in the data, and models tend to under-predict what they rarely see. \emph{SMOTE} (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique)~\cite{chawla2002} creates synthetic examples of a rare class by interpolating between real neighbours. Because \pmtwo{} is continuous, we used its regression form~\cite{torgo2013}: training rows were grouped into \pmtwo{} classes, rare classes were oversampled, and the target was interpolated along with the features. Only training rows were ever resampled.

It did not work. SMOTE made all eight models it was tried on worse on the hold-out at both strengths tested (Table~\ref{tab:smote}). Oversampling the high-pollution hours raises the average \pmtwo{} a model trains on; nothing in the inputs tells the model \emph{where} those hours belong, so it raises its predictions everywhere, while the test days were cleaner than the training period (mean 23 against 33\ugm). Cross-validation inside the training rows preferred a strength of 0.25, but that gain did not carry over to the hold-out. The final models use real rows only (\texttt{USE\_SMOTE = False}); the experiment stays in the notebook.

\subsection{Bidirectional LSTM imputation (BRITS-style)}
\label{sec:brits}

A third imputation method implements an idea proposed by one of us: a recurrent model reads each sensor series forwards and backwards, each direction estimates every value before it sees it, and a consistency loss makes the two directions agree. This is supervised learning with an added consistency loss. It follows BRITS~\cite{cao2018}: an LSTM per direction whose hidden state decays with the time since the last reading; a history-based estimate, a feature-based estimate from the other variables of the same hour, and a learned blend; a reconstruction loss on observed values only; and the forward/backward consistency loss.

Our version differs from the paper in the data (three variables from sensors that report in three short bursts), in adding the hour of day and the sensor's offline share as always-known conditioning inputs, in modelling PM2.5 on a log scale, in hiding blocks of 1 to 12 hours during training, and in having no classification head. It is written in TensorFlow; the PyPOTS library was not used because it does not report the consistency term separately or accept conditioning inputs. Scalers and the model are fitted on the training period only and each split is imputed separately. Figure~\ref{fig:britsloss} shows the training curves. Its accuracy and its effect on the models are evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:impeval}.

\subsection{Correlation of temperature, humidity and PM2.5}
\label{sec:corr}

Table~\ref{tab:corr} gives the Pearson and Spearman correlations over all sensors. Temperature and humidity are strongly and inversely related; their direct link with PM2.5 is weak. Within the five Kampala EDA sites the links are stronger and consistent in sign: PM2.5 is negatively correlated with temperature ($r$ from $-0.13$ at Nakasero to $-0.51$ at Salama Road) and positively with humidity ($r$ from $0.08$ at Namungona to $0.45$ at Kabusu).

These signs agree with the literature on how weather affects PM2.5. Warmer hours usually come with a deeper, better-mixed boundary layer that dilutes particles, while cool, humid hours at night and early morning trap them near the ground; Wang and Ogawa~\cite{wangogawa2015} found PM2.5 negatively correlated with temperature in Nagasaki. Warmth can also speed up photochemical formation of secondary particles, which pushes the other way, so the net temperature effect depends on place and season; Yang et al.~\cite{yang2017} found it negative in autumn but positive in winter across Chinese cities. Humidity has two opposing effects: moist air lets hygroscopic particles take up water and grow, and favours secondary aerosol formation, which raises PM2.5, while very humid conditions with rain remove particles by wet deposition. Which effect wins depends on particle composition and climate: Yang et al.~\cite{yang2017} found the humidity--PM2.5 correlation positive in North China but negative in most other regions, with the positive link strongest in winter and spring, and Wang and Ogawa~\cite{wangogawa2015} found the sign of the humidity relationship changing at a threshold (see also~\cite{wang2019}). With only eight days of data we cannot separate these mechanisms; the correlations are consistent with them but do not prove them.

\subsection{SMOTE: tested and left out}
\label{sec:smote}

SMOTE~\cite{chawla2002} creates synthetic examples of rare classes by interpolating between neighbours. It was not used in the earlier version. Because PM2.5 is continuous, it was applied in the way proposed for regression~\cite{torgo2013}: training rows are grouped into PM2.5 classes, the rare classes are oversampled, and the target is interpolated with the features. An air-quality example of SMOTE for PM2.5 classes is~\cite{saminathan2023}. Only training rows are ever resampled, and hyperparameters are tuned on real rows.

Table~\ref{tab:smote} shows the result on the untouched hold-out. SMOTE made all eight models worse at both strengths. Oversampling the rare high-pollution hours raises the average PM2.5 a model is trained on; the inputs cannot tell the model where those hours belong, so it raises its predictions everywhere, and the hold-out days are cleaner than the training period (mean 23 against 33\ugm). Cross-validation inside the training rows preferred a strength of 0.25, but that gain did not carry over to the hold-out for any model. The final models are therefore trained on real rows only; the experiment remains in the notebook.

We compared twelve models and a baseline:
\begin{itemize}

The \textbf{mean absolute error} (MAE) is the average size of the error in \ugm{}, and is our selection metric because it is easy to explain. The \textbf{root mean squared error} (RMSE) squares errors before averaging, so it punishes large misses more. The \textbf{coefficient of determination}, $R^2$, is the share of the variation in \pmtwo{} that the model explains: 1 is perfect, 0 is no better than predicting the mean, and negative is worse than the mean. We also report \textbf{AQI category accuracy}, the share of hours placed in the correct category. AUC and ROC curves are not used: they are measures for classifiers, and this is a regression problem.

\subsection{Does the bidirectional LSTM imputation add value?}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{As an imputer, yes.} Against the time-of-day method its error is lower in every one of the fifteen variable-and-gap combinations, by 21\% on average: about half the error for one-hour weather gaps, 15 to 27\% lower for temperature at 6 to 12 hours, and 6 to 25\% lower for PM2.5.
\item \textbf{What it adds to the real gaps: full coverage, but smoother values.} Interpolation can fill only 5 to 12\% of the values that are really missing; the time-of-day method and the bidirectional model fill all of them. The bidirectional model's fills are less varied than real readings: their standard deviation is 2.7\,\textdegree C against 4.1\,\textdegree C observed for temperature, and 11.9 against 26.8\ugm{} for PM2.5 (Figure~\ref{fig:britsfill}). It is more accurate on average because it predicts cautiously; it does not reproduce peaks.
\item \textbf{For training, no reliable gain.} Training on its weather instead of the time-of-day weather changed the prediction MAE by $-0.4\%$ on average and helped in 7 of 15 cases. It helped the LSTMs on the hold-out ($-13\%$ and $-5\%$), hurt the SVR in the expanding-window CV ($+11\%$), and changed everything else by about 1\% or less (Figure~\ref{fig:effectchange}).
\end{enumerate}

The bidirectional imputer is the right tool when the imputed values themselves are the product, especially for gaps of 6 to 12 hours. For training the PM2.5 models here it brings no reliable gain over the simpler time-of-day method, which the pipeline keeps, because weather is a minor input for these models (Section~\ref{sec:shap}).

\subsection{Decision: which imputed data the final models are trained on}

The final models, including the deployed forecast model and the models with PM2.5 history, are trained on weather filled by windowed interpolation followed by time-of-day imputation. The bidirectional LSTM imputer is kept in the notebook as a tested method, but its values are not used to train the final models, and the remaining models were not retrained on it. The reasons are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The test showed no reliable benefit.} Across five models and three validation setups the prediction MAE changed by $-0.4\%$ on average and improved in 7 of 15 cases (Figures~\ref{fig:effectmae} and~\ref{fig:effectchange}).
\item \textbf{There is little for it to improve.} The imputer fills only temperature and humidity, which carry a small share of what the models rely on (Section~\ref{sec:shap}).
\item \textbf{The one clear gain does not change the outcome.} It lowered the hold-out MAE of the LSTMs (10.89 to 9.48\ugm{} with PM2.5 history, 12.22 to 11.56\ugm{} without), but they remain behind the tree models with PM2.5 history (8.43\ugm), so the best model and the deployed model would be the same.
\item \textbf{Cost and simplicity.} The bidirectional imputer must be retrained for every data split and would add a neural network to the serving path. The time-of-day method needs no training and is easy to explain.
\item \textbf{The untested models use the same inputs.} Ridge, Linear, Polynomial, KNN and the Random Forests use the same two weather features, so a different result from retraining them is not expected. This was not tested.
\end{itemize}

Independently of this decision, PM2.5 is never imputed for training or scoring. The decision should be revisited if weather becomes a stronger predictor (for example with forecast weather for multi-day forecasting), if gaps of 6 to 12 hours become common in the data being served, or if the imputed series itself is the deliverable; in those cases the masked-gap test shows the bidirectional imputer is the better choice.

\section{Monitoring With Evidently AI}
\label{sec:monitoring}

A deployed model can silently get worse when the data it receives change. \emph{Data drift} is a change in the distribution of the inputs; \emph{model drift} is a change in the model's performance. Evidently AI~\cite{evidently} (version 0.7.23) measures both by comparing a \emph{reference} dataset, here the training period (11{,}633 rows), with a \emph{current} dataset, the test period (2{,}642 rows). The Evidently cells are wrapped so that an Evidently error cannot stop the pipeline, as the brief requires.

\textbf{What Evidently reports.} For each feature Evidently computes a \emph{drift score}, a distance between the reference and current distributions. For numeric features it uses the \emph{normed Wasserstein distance}, roughly how far the values would have to move to turn one distribution into the other, scaled by the reference spread; for categorical or low-cardinality features it uses the \emph{Jensen--Shannon distance}, a symmetric measure of how different two sets of category shares are (0 for identical). A feature is flagged as drifted when its score exceeds the threshold of 0.1. The \emph{drift share} is the fraction of features flagged, and the dataset as a whole is flagged as drifted when that share reaches 0.5.

\textbf{Data drift.} Eight of nine features drifted (share 0.89, Fig.~\ref{fig:drift}). The largest scores were day of week (1.43 and 1.19 for the sine and cosine) and month (0.65), because the test window holds two weekdays of one month; temperature and humidity drifted moderately (0.36 and 0.37) and hour of day barely (0.08 and 0.15). On this problem the alarm means ``the current data are not representative of the training data'', not ``pollution behaviour has changed''.

\Figure[t!](topskip=0pt, botskip=0pt, midskip=0pt)[width=0.98\columnwidth]{monitoring_drift_scores.png}{Evidently drift scores, training period against test period. Bars above the 0.1 threshold are flagged.\label{fig:drift}}

\textbf{Model drift.} The selected forecast model's MAE rises from 5.90\ugm{} on the reference period ($R^2 = 0.81$) to 9.34\ugm{} on the current period ($R^2 = 0.34$). Part of that rise is expected, because reference predictions are made on the rows the model was trained on. The mean prediction (22.4\ugm) stays close to the mean actual value (23.4\ugm), so the model is not systematically biased. The mean error is $-0.97$\ugm{} with a standard deviation of 15.1\ugm{}; the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), the average error as a percentage of the true value, is 44.9\%, and the largest single error is 134\ugm{}: close to unbiased on average, but able to miss individual hours badly.

\textbf{Drift against August.} A second report compares the training data with August. Six of nine features drifted: month most (0.83), since August was never seen, then day of week (0.75 and 0.61), humidity (0.26) and the mix of sensors (0.25); temperature and hour of day did not drift. This is the input-side explanation of the weak August results. In production, a drift share this high together with rising error on newly labelled readings would be the signal to retrain.

\section{Deployment: The Routes We Tried}
\label{sec:deploy}

Deployment took four attempts, and the failures shaped the final design.

\textbf{Local verification first.} Before any cloud attempt the pipeline was run end to end locally with the pinned versions in \texttt{requirements.txt} (Windows 11, Python 3.13, 16 logical CPUs, no GPU): 346 code cells with 0 errors and 70 figures, in 3{,}357\,s (about 56 minutes), with a peak memory use of 1{,}230\,MB. The forecast model file is 1.2\,MB and the nowcast model 0.6\,MB. Predicting one row takes about 14\,ms and 3{,}576 rows about 1.4\,s. A standalone inference script reproduced the notebook's predictions exactly on all 2{,}642 test rows. The model is saved with \texttt{joblib}, which \emph{serialises} it: writes the fitted pipeline, its preprocessing, the target scaler and the feature lists into one file that can be loaded elsewhere.

\textbf{Attempt 1: RunPod.} The brief proposed RunPod, a pay-as-you-go cloud service that rents GPU machines (``Pods'') by the hour, with a budget of \$5. We prepared a run script for an RTX 2000 Ada Pod. RunPod requires credit to be bought in advance, which was financially straining for us, and this route did not succeed.

\textbf{Attempt 2: Kaggle.} We next tried to run the first, \pmten{}-based notebook end to end on Kaggle's free cloud notebooks as evidence of cloud execution. This attempt did not complete.

\textbf{Attempt 3: Hugging Face.} Hugging Face hosts machine-learning models and small web apps, called \emph{Spaces}. We published the first model as the repository \texttt{Kyalis/\allowbreak airqo-pm25-\allowbreak random-forest} and its August forecasts as a Space, \texttt{Kyalis/\allowbreak airqo-pm25-\allowbreak forecasts}. Spaces that run Python on a server (Gradio, Docker or Streamlit) were refused with HTTP error 402 because they require a paid plan, so the Space is \emph{static}: it shows stored forecasts and cannot run the model. The online repository still holds the first, \pmten{}-based model.

\textbf{Attempt 4: Render and Vercel.} For the rebuilt pipeline we wanted the model itself to answer requests. We split the problem in two. \textbf{Render} runs long-lived web services; it hosts an \emph{API} (application programming interface), a web address that other programs can send requests to, around the forecast model at \url{https://airqo-cole-charis-assgnmnt.onrender.com/}. A client sends a JSON request to the \texttt{/predict} \emph{endpoint}, the specific address that performs one action, with a place (sensor), a date and time in UTC, a temperature and a humidity; the service derives the cyclical time features from the timestamp exactly as in training, runs the serialised pipeline, converts the Z-score back to \ugm{} and returns the predicted \pmtwo{} with its AQI category. Only the packages needed to load the model and predict are installed (\texttt{numpy}, \texttt{pandas}, \texttt{scikit-learn}, \texttt{scipy}, \texttt{joblib}), which suits a free instance. \textbf{Vercel} hosts the front end, a dashboard at \url{https://airqo-model.vercel.app/} that calls the Render service from the visitor's browser (Figs.~\ref{fig:dep1}--\ref{fig:dep3}).

The dashboard lets a user choose a place, a date range and an hourly or daily view; it shows the mean, maximum and minimum predicted \pmtwo{}, the predicted series over the AQI bands with the actual readings where they exist, a calendar, a map of the 143 places with coordinates, and a form that sends a request to the model and shows the exact request sent. It is honest about the model's limits: for the place in Fig.~\ref{fig:dep2} the prediction stays between about 20 and 50\ugm{} while the actual readings spike above 150\ugm{}, an MAE of 21.2\ugm{} against the August readings, in line with Section~\ref{sec:second}.

The service runs the forecast model only. The more accurate nowcast model is saved but not served, because every request would need the sensor's readings from the previous three hours. A free Render instance sleeps when idle and is slow on the first request, and the service loads a fixed model file, so retraining still means re-running the notebook and redeploying.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Table~\ref{tab:decisions} summarises the route from the first pipeline to the final one. Two lessons stand out. The first is that an excellent score deserves suspicion: the Phase~1 model's MAE of 3.10\ugm{} came from a co-measurement of the target, and only by asking what the model relied on did we see that it could not forecast. The second is that, on records this short, the simplest strong signal wins. Recent \pmtwo{} was worth more than any amount of care over the weather inputs: the most accurate imputer we built changed prediction error by less than half a percent, while three hours of history cut it by 12--14\% on the hold-out and kept the models useful on a new month.

\textbf{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Eight days of data.} Three two-day bursts cannot show seasonal patterns, and the two-day hold-out is short; the three setups and the August test are needed to judge stability.
\item \textbf{Weak predictors without \pmten{}.} Temperature and humidity carry only 3--8\% of the SHAP attribution here. Eight days are too few for a model to learn the weather effects described in the literature~\cite{wangogawa2015,yang2017,wabinyai2026}.
\item \textbf{Sensor accuracy.} The impossible \pmtwo{}$>$\pmten{} readings point to calibration error, which the models learn as it is~\cite{adong2022,lsoto2026}.
\item \textbf{Extreme values.} Replacing them lowers the reported errors (about 1.3\ugm{} against the untouched readings), and the replacement was not applied to the August file.
\item \textbf{The imputation decision} was measured for five models only.
\item \textbf{Deployment.} The required RunPod run with logs was not achieved; the live service runs the forecast model on free tiers, and the published Hugging Face model is still the Phase~1 version.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Future work.} The largest gain would come from training on continuous data, such as the August file. Beyond that: combine lagged pollutant features with multi-step forecasting and forecast weather; use same-hour \pmtwo{} from neighbouring sensors, which are dense around Kampala; move the service to a managed cloud platform such as Google Cloud (Cloud Run or a Vertex AI endpoint) or Azure (Azure Machine Learning online endpoints or Container Apps) for autoscaling and model versioning; export the model to ONNX, an open format that lets a model run in a web browser, so a static page could run it; and automate monitoring, with scheduled Evidently reports, retraining when error grows and rollback to the previous model if a retrained one validates worse.

%==================================================================== X
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We built and deployed a reproducible pipeline that predicts hourly \pmtwo{} from AirQo sensor data containing only eight days of readings. Our first model looked excellent because it used \pmten{}, a same-hour co-measurement of the target; removing it gave an honest forecast model, a tuned SVR with a hold-out MAE of 9.34\ugm{}, and nowcast models with three hours of \pmtwo{} history that reached 8.43\ugm{} and stayed useful on a later, full month. We tried and rejected KNN-invented coordinates and long-gap fills, SMOTE oversampling and, for training, our most accurate imputer, each on measured evidence. The model is served by an API on Render with a dashboard on Vercel after RunPod and Kaggle attempts failed and Hugging Face could host only a static page. In a sparse network, recent pollutant history matters more than weather or sophisticated imputation; more continuous data, not more complex models, is the next step.

\section{Model monitoring with Evidently AI}

Evidently 0.7.23~\cite{evidently} compares a \emph{reference} dataset, the training period (11{,}633 rows), with a \emph{current} dataset, the test period (2{,}642 rows). The Evidently cells are wrapped so that an Evidently error cannot crash the pipeline, as the brief requires.

\textbf{Phase 1 monitoring.} In the first pipeline Evidently flagged 6 of 7 scored columns as drifted. Those seven were the time features and the sensor identity; temperature, humidity and PM10 were not scored, most likely because they were held in the nullable \texttt{Float64} type, which that report did not pick up. The drift came from the make-up of the two windows (different months, weekdays and active sensors), not from a change in the air. In the revised pipeline temperature and humidity are among the nine scored features below.

\section{Deployment}

The pipeline is deployed in three parts: the training pipeline is the notebook, verified end to end (Section~\ref{sec:local}); the trained model is served as a web service on \textbf{Render}; and a dashboard that uses that service is hosted on \textbf{Vercel}.

\subsection{Deployment history and choice of platforms}

The deployment changed several times. In order:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{RunPod (planned).} The brief proposed RunPod, a pay-as-you-go GPU cloud. We prepared to run the pipeline on an RTX 2000 Ada Pod with a run script and a prediction endpoint. RunPod requires credit to be bought in advance, which became financially straining for the two of us, so this plan was dropped.
\item \textbf{Kaggle (cloud execution).} The first, PM10-based version of the notebook was run end to end on Kaggle's cloud notebook service as the evidence of cloud execution, with a run-log section that records the runtime, hardware, row counts, metrics and output files.
\item \textbf{Hugging Face (model and static demo).} The first model was published as the model repository \texttt{Kyalis/airqo-pm25-random-forest}, and its August forecasts as a static demo Space, \texttt{Kyalis/airqo-pm25-forecasts}. Server-side Spaces (Gradio, Docker or Streamlit) were refused with HTTP 402 because they need a paid PRO plan, so the demo only displays stored forecasts and does not run the model. Running the model in the browser with ONNX would avoid this and is left as future work.
\item \textbf{Render and Vercel (final).} For the revised pipeline we wanted the model itself to answer requests. The model runs as a web service on Render and the dashboard is hosted on Vercel (Sections~\ref{sec:render} and~\ref{sec:vercel}).
\end{enumerate}

Render and Vercel both have free tiers and fit the two halves of the problem:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Render} runs a long-lived Python web service, which is what loading a scikit-learn model and predicting on request needs.
\item \textbf{Vercel} hosts the front end, a web dashboard that calls the Render service from the visitor's browser.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Model service on Render}
\label{sec:render}

The saved forecast model (\texttt{best\_pm25\_model.joblib}: the SVR pipeline with its preprocessing, the target scaler and the feature lists) is served by a small Python web service on Render at \addurl{Render service URL}.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Small footprint.} The service only needs the packages required to load the model and predict. They are pinned in \texttt{requirements-render.txt}: \texttt{numpy}, \texttt{pandas}, \texttt{scikit-learn}, \texttt{scipy} and \texttt{joblib}. TensorFlow, LightGBM, SHAP and the mapping libraries are needed for training and analysis only, so they are not installed on the server. The model file is 1.2 MB and a single prediction takes about 10\,ms, which suits a free instance.
\item \textbf{Interface.} The service accepts a JSON \texttt{POST} request to \texttt{/predict} with the place (sensor), the date and time in UTC, the temperature and the humidity, and returns the predicted PM2.5 in \ugm{} together with its AQI category. The time features (hour, day of week and month as sine and cosine pairs) are derived from the timestamp, exactly as in training, and the prediction is transformed back from the Z-score scale with the saved target scaler.
\item \textbf{Same code path as the notebook.} The service uses the serialised pipeline unchanged, so preprocessing is identical to training. The standalone inference script reproduced the notebook's predictions exactly on all 2{,}642 test rows.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations of the deployment}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Forecast model only.} The service runs the forecast model (SVR). The more accurate model with PM2.5 history is saved but not served, because it needs the sensor's readings from the previous three hours with every request.
\item \textbf{Free tiers.} A free Render instance stops when idle and takes time to start again on the first request, and it has limited memory, which is why the service installs only the small set of packages above.
\item \textbf{No automatic retraining.} The service loads a fixed model file. Retraining still means running the notebook and redeploying the new file.
\item \textbf{Earlier deployments.} The Kaggle run and the Hugging Face model card and static demo were made with the first, PM10-based version of the pipeline and do not reflect the revised model.
\end{itemize}

%==================================================================== 14
\section{Discussion and limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Little data in time.} Eight days in three bursts cannot capture seasonal patterns. The three validation setups disagree on the ranking of the forecast models, and the external August test, on 125 of the same sensors, shows that the forecast models do not transfer to a new month.
\item \textbf{Short test window.} The chronological hold-out is two days (2--3 June); the expanding-window and leave-sensors-out setups and the August test are needed to judge stability.
\item \textbf{Weak predictors without PM10.} With PM10 the earlier Random Forest scored MAE 3.10\ugm{} ($R^2$ 0.90), but that score came from a co-measurement of the same particles, read in the same hour, rather than from what drives PM2.5 (Section~\ref{sec:evolution}). PM10 is also the strongest predictor in G\"urtepe et al.~\cite{gurtepe2025}, which shows how closely the two measurements track each other; it does not make PM10 a cause of PM2.5. Removing it is the right choice for forecasting, but on our data the drivers that remain explain little: temperature and humidity carry only 3--8\% of the SHAP attribution (Table~\ref{tab:shap}). Eight days are too few for the model to learn the weather effects that the literature describes, and the recent PM2.5 readings are the only strong signal available.
\item \textbf{Sensor calibration.} PM2.5 exceeded PM10 in 22.4\% of the raw rows and some PM10 readings were below 1\ugm, which point to calibration problems in the low-cost sensors. The models learn from these readings as they are, apart from the extreme-value replacement.
\item \textbf{Forecast and nowcast are different tasks.} The models with PM2.5 history are the most accurate but need recent readings from the sensor, so they cannot produce the two-week forecast.
\item \textbf{Extreme values.} Replacing extreme readings lowers the reported errors; scored against the untouched readings the best models' MAEs are about 1.3\ugm{} higher. The replacement rule uses each sensor's whole record, and it was not applied to the second dataset.
\item \textbf{SMOTE} was evaluated and rejected on evidence; the cross-validation used to choose its strength was itself unstable because the folds come from different bursts.
\item \textbf{Imputation.} The bidirectional imputer's PM2.5 fills are too smooth to serve as training targets, and its benefit for prediction is negligible on this data. Only five models were retrained on its values; Ridge, Linear, Polynomial, KNN and the Random Forests were not retested, so the conclusion for them is an expectation, not a measurement.
\item \textbf{Deployment.} The live service runs the forecast model only, on free hosting tiers; the model with PM2.5 history is not served.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future development}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{More days of data.} The largest improvement would come from training on continuous data, such as the August file, instead of three two-day bursts.
\item \textbf{Forecasting from history.} Combine lagged pollutant features with multi-step forecasting, feeding each prediction forward, and add forecast weather.
\item \textbf{Neighbouring sensors.} Use same-hour PM2.5 from nearby sensors, which the maps show are dense around Kampala.
\item \textbf{Managed cloud deployment.} Move the model service to Google Cloud (Cloud Run or a Vertex AI endpoint) or Azure (Azure Machine Learning online endpoints or Container Apps), which add autoscaling and model versioning.
\item \textbf{In-browser inference.} Export the model to ONNX so that a static page, such as the Hugging Face Space, can run it without a server.
\item \textbf{Automated monitoring}, with scheduled Evidently reports, retraining when error grows, and rollback to the previous model if a retrain validates worse.
\end{itemize}

%==================================================================== 15
\section{Conclusion}

We delivered a reproducible pipeline that cleans the AirQo data without inventing PM2.5 targets, removes PM10, profiles sensors by offline time, fills gaps with three methods that were tested against hidden values, handles extreme readings, and compares thirteen models under three validation setups, with SHAP explanations, sensor maps and an external test. The best forecast model, a tuned SVR, reaches MAE 9.34\ugm{} on held-out data; tree models that also use the last three hours of PM2.5 reach 8.43\ugm{} and are far more stable. SMOTE and the bidirectional LSTM imputer were both implemented and tested: SMOTE made every model worse and was left out, and the bidirectional imputer is a better imputer but does not improve prediction. The main lesson is that, without PM10, the recent PM2.5 readings are the only strong predictor in this data, and that models trained on eight days do not transfer to a new month unless they are given those readings.
