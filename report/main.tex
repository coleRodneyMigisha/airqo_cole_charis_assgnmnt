%-------------------------------------------------------------------- Abstract
\begin{abstract}
We built a complete pipeline that predicts hourly PM2.5 concentration from AirQo low-cost sensor data: data preparation, exploratory analysis, model training, evaluation, explainability, an external test on a second dataset, August 2026 forecasts, monitoring with Evidently AI, and deployment. The supplied data contains 14{,}600 hourly records from 149 sensors, but readings exist on only eight days (1--3 January, 1--2 April and 1--3 June 2026). Following feedback on our presentation, PM10 was removed from the pipeline, so PM2.5 is predicted from temperature, humidity, time and sensor identity. Gaps are filled by windowed interpolation and time-of-day imputation, and a bidirectional LSTM imputer in the style of BRITS was added and tested. Extreme PM2.5 readings (1.5\% of readings) are replaced by the nearest normal reading of the same sensor. SMOTE was tested on the training data and left out because it made every model less accurate. Thirteen models were compared under three validation setups. The best forecast model, a tuned SVR, reaches MAE 9.34\ugm{} ($R^2$ 0.34) on the held-out June days. Adding the PM2.5 readings of the previous three hours to the tree models lowers this to 8.43\ugm{} ($R^2$ 0.42) and makes the models far more stable, but such models need recent readings and cannot forecast weeks ahead. On an external August dataset the forecast models are no better than predicting the training mean, while the models with PM2.5 history keep part of their skill (MAE 14.8\ugm). The bidirectional imputer is the most accurate imputer for gaps of six hours or more, but it does not improve the prediction models. With PM10 as an input the earlier version of the pipeline scored MAE 3.10\ugm; that accuracy depended on a second particle reading from the same instrument.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

AirQo, based at Makerere University, operates a network of locally manufactured low-cost air-quality monitors across African cities. Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) is the pollutant of greatest health concern: the particles penetrate deep into the lungs and bloodstream, and a four-year reference-grade study cited in the assignment brief found Kampala's annual average to be about 39\ugm{}, roughly eight times the WHO annual guideline of 5\ugm{}~\cite{who2021}. Forecasting PM2.5 supports early warnings for the public and health services, and planning by NEMA and the ministries responsible for energy and transport.

The assignment asks for a deployable pipeline that goes from raw sensor data to a trained, evaluated, monitored and cloud-deployed model. This report documents each stage, the decisions taken and the evidence behind them. It describes the revised pipeline that followed the feedback on our presentation. The main changes are listed in Section~\ref{sec:changes}. The AQI categories used throughout are those of Section~4 of the brief (Table~\ref{tab:aqi}).

\subsection{What changed after the presentation}
\label{sec:changes}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{PM10 removed} from every table, plot, feature set and model.
\item \textbf{Sensors profiled by offline time} and classed as data-rich or data-poor.
\item \textbf{Gap filling in two steps:} windowed interpolation, then time-of-day imputation; plus a \textbf{bidirectional LSTM imputer} (BRITS-style) proposed by a team member.
\item \textbf{Extreme PM2.5 values} replaced by the nearest normal reading of the same sensor.
\item \textbf{SMOTE} tested on the training split, documented, and left out of the final models.
\item \textbf{New models:} LightGBM, an LSTM, and tree models that also use the PM2.5 readings of the previous hours.
\item \textbf{Three validation setups} instead of one.
\item \textbf{New evaluation:} masked-gap imputation test, SHAP explanations, sensor maps, and an external test on a second dataset.
\item \textbf{Plots revised:} calendars coloured by PM2.5 category, density plots instead of histograms, one combined time-series figure per site, and no AUC (it is not a regression metric).
\end{itemize}

\section{Data}

The dataset (\texttt{Sensor Data Jan - July 2026.csv}) contains 14{,}600 hourly records with 12 fields: site name, timestamp, sample frequency, network (AirQo or AirGradient), latitude, longitude, temperature, humidity, calibrated PM2.5 and PM10, site ID and device name. It covers 149 devices. There are no duplicate rows and no duplicate device-hour pairs, so \texttt{device\_name} together with the timestamp uniquely identifies an observation.

Three properties of the data shaped every later decision:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Very sparse coverage in time.} Although the file is named January--July, readings exist on only eight days, in three short windows: 1--3 January, 1--2 April and 1--3 June 2026. That is 147 hours in which at least one sensor reported.
\item \textbf{Zero values that are not real readings.} The brief defines 0 or empty as missing. Temperature is zero or empty in 4{,}120 rows and humidity in 4{,}365; PM2.5 is empty in 325.
\item \textbf{Uneven sensors.} Devices contribute between 20 and 147 records (median 105).
\end{enumerate}

A second file, \texttt{AirQo\_August\_2026\_hourly\_matched\_partial.csv}, became available later and is used only as an external test set (Section~\ref{sec:second}).

\section{Data preparation}

\subsection{Approach and supporting literature}

The preparation follows the general workflow of Febrian and Permai~\cite{febrian2025}: standardise null values, quantify missingness, explore distributions and outliers, analyse correlations, impute where justified, normalise, and split chronologically. AirQo's own studies describe the characteristics we found in this data: intermittent device availability, varying data completeness, and calibrated values from low-cost sensors~\cite{okure2022,sserunjogi2022}. PM2.5 \emph{targets} are never imputed for training or scoring, because that would create artificial labels.

\subsection{Cleaning}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{PM10} is dropped immediately after loading.
\item \textbf{Zeros} in temperature, humidity, PM2.5, latitude and longitude are converted to missing. The zero temperature and humidity readings (about 4{,}100 rows) are a weather-sensor dropout: every zero temperature coincides with a zero humidity, they come in long unbroken blocks, and they occur only on AirQo devices.
\item \textbf{Types:} timestamps are parsed as UTC, and \texttt{device\_name} is used as the sensor identifier because \texttt{site\_id} is missing in about half the rows.
\item \textbf{Empty sensors:} a sensor is removed if it has no valid reading at all or no valid PM2.5 reading. None met this rule, so none was dropped. Nine sensors report PM2.5 but never report weather; they were kept and their weather is imputed.
\item \textbf{Coordinates} are filled with the median of the site's own valid coordinates. Six sensors have no valid coordinate and are left without one.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Extreme PM2.5 values}
\label{sec:extreme}

A few PM2.5 readings are far outside anything else the same sensor reports; the largest is 998\ugm. A reading is treated as extreme when it is above the sensor's own far-out limit, $Q_3 + 3\,\mathrm{IQR}$. Each extreme reading is replaced by the nearest reading \emph{in time} from the \emph{same sensor} that is within the normal range, looking at most six hours away; if none exists, the sensor's median normal reading is used.

This flagged 225 of 14{,}275 readings (1.5\%) at 59 sensors. 223 were replaced by a normal reading at most five hours away (median one hour) and two by the sensor median. The mean of PM2.5 moves from 32.7 to 31.6\ugm, the standard deviation from 30.4 to 26.8\ugm, and the maximum from 998 to 415\ugm{} (Figure~\ref{fig:extreme}). The original values are kept, and Section~\ref{sec:results} also reports the models' error against the untouched readings.

\subsection{Windowed interpolation}

A missing hour is filled by linear interpolation only when the gap is at most three hours long, has valid readings on both sides, and the 24-hour window centred on it holds at least six real readings. It runs separately for each sensor, and the window, gap limit and minimum count are parameters. Longer gaps stay missing. It filled 555 temperature and 551 humidity values.

\subsection{Time-of-day imputation}

Longer gaps inside the observation windows are estimated from the daily cycle. The mean and variance of each variable are computed per hour of day for each sensor, falling back to the sensor's network group and then to all sensors when a sensor has too few readings. The sensor's own readings within 12 hours of the gap give a local offset from that profile, and the missing value is the profile value plus the offset (Figure~\ref{fig:diurnal}). For the models, the profiles are refitted on training rows only.

\subsection{Bidirectional LSTM imputation (BRITS-style)}
\label{sec:brits}

A third imputation method implements an idea from a team member: a recurrent model reads each sensor series forwards and backwards, each direction estimates every value before it sees it, and a consistency loss makes the two directions agree. This is supervised learning with an added consistency loss. It follows BRITS~\cite{cao2018}: an LSTM per direction whose hidden state decays with the time since the last reading; a history-based estimate, a feature-based estimate from the other variables of the same hour, and a learned blend; a reconstruction loss on observed values only; and the forward/backward consistency loss.

Our version differs from the paper in the data (three variables from sensors that report in three short bursts), in adding the hour of day and the sensor's offline share as always-known conditioning inputs, in modelling PM2.5 on a log scale, in hiding blocks of 1 to 12 hours during training, and in having no classification head. It is written in TensorFlow; the PyPOTS library was not used because it does not report the consistency term separately or accept conditioning inputs. Scalers and the model are fitted on the training period only and each split is imputed separately. Figure~\ref{fig:britsloss} shows the training curves. Its accuracy and its effect on the models are evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:impeval}.

\subsection{Features}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Weather:} temperature and humidity.
\item \textbf{Time:} hour of day, day of week and month, each as a sine/cosine pair.
\item \textbf{Sensor identity:} \texttt{device\_name}, one-hot encoded.
\item \textbf{PM2.5 history} (for the models that use it): the readings of the same sensor 1, 2 and 3 hours earlier, their mean, and how many of them exist. Only real earlier readings are used, so the value being predicted cannot leak into its own inputs.
\item \textbf{Not used:} PM10, coordinates (the device already identifies the location) and administrative fields.
\end{itemize}

\section{Model implementation}

\subsection{Set-up}
\label{sec:setup}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Rows:} the 14{,}275 hours with a PM2.5 reading.
\item \textbf{Target:} the Z-score of PM2.5, fitted on training rows only; predictions are transformed back to \ugm.
\item \textbf{Preprocessing}, inside each pipeline: numeric inputs standardised, a remaining missing value filled with the training median, \texttt{device\_name} one-hot encoded with unseen devices ignored.
\item \textbf{Tuning:} grid or randomised search with \texttt{TimeSeriesSplit} on the real training rows.
\item \textbf{Metrics:} MAE (the selection metric), RMSE and $R^2$, plus AQI category accuracy. AUC is not used because it is not a regression metric.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{SMOTE: tested and left out}
\label{sec:smote}

SMOTE~\cite{chawla2002} creates synthetic examples of rare classes by interpolating between neighbours. It was not used in the earlier version. Because PM2.5 is continuous, it was applied in the way proposed for regression~\cite{torgo2013}: training rows are grouped into PM2.5 classes, the rare classes are oversampled, and the target is interpolated with the features. An air-quality example of SMOTE for PM2.5 classes is~\cite{saminathan2023}. Only training rows are ever resampled, and hyperparameters are tuned on real rows.

Table~\ref{tab:smote} shows the result on the untouched hold-out. SMOTE made all eight models worse at both strengths. Oversampling the rare high-pollution hours raises the average PM2.5 a model is trained on; the inputs cannot tell the model where those hours belong, so it raises its predictions everywhere, and the hold-out days are cleaner than the training period (mean 23 against 33\ugm). Cross-validation inside the training rows preferred a strength of 0.25, but that gain did not carry over to the hold-out for any model. The final models are therefore trained on real rows only; the experiment remains in the notebook.

\subsection{Models}

Thirteen models were compared:
\begin{itemize}
\item the six from the earlier version, trained with scikit-learn~\cite{pedregosa2011}: Linear, Polynomial and Ridge regression, Random Forest~\cite{breiman2001} (untuned and tuned), SVR and KNN;
\item \textbf{LightGBM}, a gradient-boosted tree model, with a light random search;
\item \textbf{two LSTMs} on six-hour sliding windows per sensor, one with weather and time only and one that also sees the PM2.5 reading of the previous hour at each step, with early stopping (Figure~\ref{fig:lstmloss});
\item \textbf{Random Forest and LightGBM with PM2.5 history}, the tree models with the history features of Section~4;
\item a baseline that always predicts the training mean.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Does the bidirectional LSTM imputation add value?}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{As an imputer, yes.} Against the time-of-day method its error is lower in every one of the fifteen variable-and-gap combinations, by 21\% on average: about half the error for one-hour weather gaps, 15 to 27\% lower for temperature at 6 to 12 hours, and 6 to 25\% lower for PM2.5.
\item \textbf{What it adds to the real gaps: full coverage, but smoother values.} Interpolation can fill only 5 to 12\% of the values that are really missing; the time-of-day method and the bidirectional model fill all of them. The bidirectional model's fills are less varied than real readings: their standard deviation is 2.7\,\textdegree C against 4.1\,\textdegree C observed for temperature, and 11.9 against 26.8\ugm{} for PM2.5 (Figure~\ref{fig:britsfill}). It is more accurate on average because it predicts cautiously; it does not reproduce peaks.
\item \textbf{For training, no reliable gain.} Training on its weather instead of the time-of-day weather changed the prediction MAE by $-0.4\%$ on average and helped in 7 of 15 cases. It helped the LSTMs on the hold-out ($-13\%$ and $-5\%$), hurt the SVR in the expanding-window CV ($+11\%$), and changed everything else by less than 1\%.
\end{enumerate}

The bidirectional imputer is the right tool when the imputed values themselves are the product, especially for gaps of 6 to 12 hours. For training the PM2.5 models here it brings no reliable gain over the simpler time-of-day method, which the pipeline keeps, because weather is a minor input for these models (Section~\ref{sec:shap}).

Without PM2.5 history, the tree models rely on when and where a reading was taken, not on the weather (Table~\ref{tab:shap}, Figure~\ref{fig:shapbee}). \section{Model monitoring with Evidently AI}

Evidently 0.7.23~\cite{evidently} compares a \emph{reference} dataset, the training period (11{,}633 rows), with a \emph{current} dataset, the test period (2{,}642 rows). The Evidently cells are wrapped so that an Evidently error cannot crash the pipeline, as the brief requires.

\textbf{Data drift.} For each feature Evidently reports a drift score: the normed Wasserstein distance for numeric features and the Jensen--Shannon distance for categorical or low-cardinality features, with a threshold of 0.1. Eight of nine features drifted (share 0.89). The largest scores are day of week (1.43 and 1.19) and month (0.65), because the test window holds only two weekdays of one month; temperature and humidity drifted moderately (0.36 and 0.37) and hour of day barely (0.08 and 0.15). The alert means ``the current data is not representative of what the model was trained on'' rather than ``pollution behaviour has changed''.

\section{Discussion and limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Little data in time.} Eight days in three bursts cannot capture seasonal patterns. The three validation setups disagree on the ranking of the forecast models, and the external August test shows that the forecast models do not transfer to a new month.
\item \textbf{Weak predictors without PM10.} With PM10 the earlier Random Forest scored MAE 3.10\ugm{} ($R^2$ 0.90). Without it, weather, time and sensor identity explain little of hourly PM2.5; the recent PM2.5 readings are the only strong signal available.
\item \textbf{Forecast and nowcast are different tasks.} The models with PM2.5 history are the most accurate but need recent readings from the sensor, so they cannot produce the two-week forecast.
\item \textbf{Extreme values.} Replacing extreme readings lowers the reported errors; scored against the untouched readings the best models' MAEs are about 1.3\ugm{} higher. The replacement rule uses each sensor's whole record, and it was not applied to the second dataset.
\item \textbf{SMOTE} was evaluated and rejected on evidence; the cross-validation used to choose its strength was itself unstable because the folds come from different bursts.
\item \textbf{Imputation.} The bidirectional imputer's PM2.5 fills are too smooth to serve as training targets, and its benefit for prediction is negligible on this data.
\item \textbf{Deployment.} The cloud run and the public demo still reflect the earlier version and must be redone.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future development}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{More days of data.} The largest improvement would come from training on continuous data, such as the August file, instead of three two-day bursts.
\item \textbf{Forecasting from history.} Combine the PM2.5 history features with multi-step forecasting, feeding each prediction forward, and add forecast weather.
\item \textbf{Neighbouring sensors.} Use same-hour PM2.5 from nearby sensors, which the maps show are dense around Kampala.
\item \textbf{Managed cloud deployment and automated monitoring}, with scheduled Evidently reports, retraining when error grows, and rollback to the previous model if a retrain validates worse.
\end{itemize}

%==================================================================== 15
\section{Conclusion}

We delivered a reproducible pipeline that cleans the AirQo data without inventing PM2.5 targets, removes PM10, profiles sensors by offline time, fills gaps with three methods that were tested against hidden values, handles extreme readings, and compares thirteen models under three validation setups, with SHAP explanations, sensor maps and an external test. The best forecast model, a tuned SVR, reaches MAE 9.34\ugm{} on held-out data; tree models that also use the last three hours of PM2.5 reach 8.43\ugm{} and are far more stable. SMOTE and the bidirectional LSTM imputer were both implemented and tested: SMOTE made every model worse and was left out, and the bidirectional imputer is a better imputer but does not improve prediction. The main lesson is that, without PM10, the recent PM2.5 readings are the only strong predictor in this data, and that models trained on eight days do not transfer to a new month unless they are given those readings.
